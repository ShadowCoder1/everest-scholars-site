What 148 ISEF winners had in common.

We went back through the winners of ISEF's biggest prizes, all the way to 1995. That's 119 people. Then we added 29 more winners who told their stories in detail. Award lists, news stories, talks, blog posts: more than 500 sources. These four patterns kept showing up.

Most winning ideas we could trace started in the student's own life, not on a list.

For 56 of the winning projects, we could trace where the idea came from. 35 started with something from the student's own life: a hobby, a person they cared about, a problem where they lived. 9 started with something they read or watched. 12 came out of a class, a lab or a program. None came from a list of project ideas.

The details are often small and specific. Krithik Ramesh won a $75,000 top prize in 2019, at 16, for a system designed to help surgeons place screws in the spine.1 He had read about how often those screws end up misplaced, and the method came from a dance game. In his words: "My project started because I was trying to like five star Shakira's 'Hips Don't Lie' on Just Dance."23

Rachel Seevers noticed white streaks over a plane wing and realized she had no idea how airplanes flew. That curiosity became a five-year project.4 One winner built a window-cleaning robot after a man fell while cleaning a glass awning.5 Another was inspired by his blind father.6

What to do with this

Start with the thing you can't stop noticing: a problem in your family, your town or your hobby. Then read what has already been tried. A topic picked off a list is hard to care about for a full season.

Coming back is normal. Many top winners were on their second or third trip.

In a typical year, about one in six ISEF finalists has been to ISEF before.78 Among the top-award winners since 2008, it is at least one in three: 23 of the 70 we could check. That's about double.

George Hotz made it to ISEF at 14 and left without a grand award. The next year he placed third in his category.910 Rachel Seevers was a finalist in 2017, won a grand award in 2018, and on her fourth trip in 2019 won a $50,000 top award.1112

What to do with this

Plan for two seasons, not one. Many top winners were back for a second or third try. Treat the first season's judges as feedback for the next one.

Half of earlier winners worked in a university lab. Most recent winners didn't.

Between 2008 and 2016, half of the winners we could place did their work in a university or research lab. Since 2017, it's 4 out of 19, plus one who split her time between a lab and home. The rest worked at home or in a garage (5), at school (1), or entirely on a computer (8).

Robert Sansone won a $75,000 top award in 2022 for an electric-car motor designed without rare-earth magnets. He said he didn't really have a mentor, and that the 15th motor he built was the first one that worked.1314 In 2011, Blake Marggraff and Matthew Feddersen won the top prize for a project done "completely in the back of a public high school classroom with about $200 of materials," after placing second at their state fair.1516

What to do with this

Don't wait for a lab to start. Many strong projects are computational or built at home. One rule to know early: the research plan comes before any experiment, and projects with human participants, vertebrate animals or hazardous biological agents need review-board approval before you begin.17

The ones who found a scientist usually asked, a lot.

From 2008 to 2016, the years with the best records, 30 of the 36 winners we studied had a mentor: a professor, a researcher or a teacher. The ones who found a scientist often didn't get one through connections. They went and asked.

  • Jack Andraka wrote to 200 professors. One said, "Maybe I might be able to help you, kid." That maybe turned into lab space at Johns Hopkins, and in 2012 he won the top prize.18
  • Ari Dyckovsky and his teacher sent more than 60 letters to scientists. One responded.19
  • Nithin Tumma emailed 60 or 70 people. One said yes.20

What to do with this

Expect a lot of no's, and a lot of silence. You only need one maybe. Write short, specific emails that show you have read the person's work.

What this means for this season.

Most US science fairs run from January through March.21 If a project is already underway, the next few weeks are about the paper, the board and the judging interview. If you're starting from a question, a project begun this winter realistically targets next year's fair season, and that's fine. Many top winners took more than one.

One new rule matters for everyone: ISEF now asks every student to fill out a Student Support Disclosure Form (2A), listing any paid support such as mentorship.22 If you work with any paid mentor, including us, you list it. Good help is easy to disclose because the student does the work.

About this guide

We run a mentorship company, so we have a bias. That's why every claim links to its source. None of the winners named here worked with Everest Futures. Their stories are public, and we use them only to show patterns.

Who we counted: 119 winners of ISEF's top awards from 1995 to 2026 (the Young Scientist, Moore, Yancopoulos and Leighton-Berger awards, and before 2008 also the Seaborg trip award), plus 6 Best of Category winners and 23 winners who wrote or spoke about their projects in detail. The 2020 fair was cancelled. Where a source didn't say something, we counted it as unknown rather than guess, which is why some counts cover fewer than 148 people. Data checked in September 2026.
